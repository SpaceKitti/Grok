It's fun to think about what classical gravity would look like in other dimensions. In 3D, gravity satisfies Poisson's equation. For a point source this says that the gravitational potential goes like 1/r and the force like 1/r^2. In 2D, a point source gives a potential like -log(r) and a force 1/r. What would this look like in a domain with periodic boundary conditions?
Well, the question is a bit self-contradictory. Poisson's equation can be thought of as solving for the time independent flow of water, given a few sources. If you're in a finite domain... and you're constantly adding water... there's no "time independent" part, you just keep filling up the domain with more water. But it's fun to see what happens when we try to force the matter, so let's just keep adding point sources until there's a point source at every point on a grid. 
This is what the potential looks like for two point sources. So far so good. I define the point (0,0) to have zero potential, and I'm free to do this because we can always subtract a constant from the potential energy without affecting the force. (or, we can always subtract a constant from the potential without changing the flow of water)
 
After this, the limit as n goes to infinity can be taken. The magic of complex analysis helps us out here to give a simple closed form expression.
 
The issue is that this plot shows, in the water analogy, there's a constant flow away from the point sources no matter how far you get. This can't be subtracted away. If we try to add up a bunch of lines of point sources now, we get this funky shape... in fact this isn't even well-defined any more, if we summed up our infinite grid of point sources any other way, we'd get a different result.
 
okay... but hold on, this looks remarkably like a parabola, right? Every point source dumps in 2*pi gallons of water per second. So what if we remove a uniform amount of water from all of space, at the rate of 2*pi gallons per second per meter squared? In this case it just means adding the function +pi*y^2... and oh shoot, it looks like we've converged to something:
 
We can compare to a derivation with a bit less of a dubious background (Fundamental solution of the Laplacian on flat tori [...]) and-- aha! The proper way to write this is in terms of an elliptic theta function!
